## What this section has to do

Make it easy for somebody to say yes, and then to write a strong letter quickly.

This is the one document in the set you do not get to write. The recommender writes the letter; you supply facts they can check and a deadline they can meet. Nothing below asserts what they saw, supervised, or thought of you, because you are not the one who saw it — every sentence about your shared work is a blank you fill from your own records, and every judgement about that work stays theirs. Write what you can document — the term, the project, the callsign, the count, the piece of equipment — and let them decide what they are willing to say about it.

## Before you send

1. **What kind of recommender does this program require?** Read the funder's own published criteria, or the overlay for that program if this app ships one. Programs differ on how many letters they want and on who counts: some require a member or an officer of a named organization, some require a faculty member, some accept any references at all. Guessing here wastes their time and yours.
2. **Who are you asking?** {{recommender.name}} — {{recommender.role}}. Write the role as *they* would describe it, and check it against what the program requires before you send.
3. **Does the letter come back to you, or go straight to the funder?** If there is a portal, find the link before you write, not after they agree.
4. **How much notice are you giving?** Count the days. If the answer embarrasses you, say so in the first line rather than hoping they will not notice.

## Skeleton

Subject: Reference letter for {{funder.programName}} — due {{recommender.deadline}}

Dear {{recommender.name}},

> One sentence: what you are applying for, and the amount or award only if the funder publishes one.

I am applying for {{funder.programName}} and the reference is due {{recommender.deadline}}. Would you be willing to write a letter of reference?

> One short paragraph reminding them of specific shared work: the dates, the course or project, and what you did. Never assume they remember, and never draft their opinion for them.

To make this quick, I have attached:

- the program's published criteria — {{funder.applyUrl}}, or where the funder publishes no link, the contact they do publish: {{funder.applyContact}}
- a one-page summary of what I have done, with dates and counts
- the exact submission instructions and the deadline

> If they submit through a portal rather than sending the letter to you, say so here and give the link.

Thank you either way — a "no" now is more useful to me than a late yes.

{{student.name}} ({{student.callsign}})

> Asking on behalf of a club rather than for yourself? Sign as {{team.leadName}}, {{team.leadRole}}, {{club.name}}, and say which of you the letter should be about.

## Common failure

Asking with three days' notice and no supporting facts. A recommender reconstructing your accomplishments from memory writes a warm, vague letter. A recommender handed dates and numbers writes a specific one.

The quieter failure is sending them a draft letter to sign. It puts words in their mouth, and a letter you wrote is not a reference — it is your own essay with somebody else's name at the bottom. Send facts, not sentences.
