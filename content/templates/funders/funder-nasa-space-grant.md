## There is no national deadline

NASA's National Space Grant College and Fellowship Program runs through **52 independent consortia** — one for each state, plus the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico. Each runs its own calendar, its own award types, its own forms and its own website. There is no national application and **no national deadline**, which is why this programme cannot be a feed and is a picker instead.

For a collegiate amateur radio group this is often the most realistic route to a ground station, a balloon payload or a cubesat: the science framing is already native to the hobby, and consortia are explicitly looking for student projects. As an indication of scale only — GrantSpotter's own reading of the sector, and not a published figure from NASA or from any consortium — student awards at consortium level commonly fall somewhere between $1,000 and $10,000.

## What to do, in order

1. Open the NASA directory at nasa.gov, find your state's consortium, and follow it to its own site.
2. Find its **student opportunities** or **research infrastructure** page and note every award type it runs, each with its own closing date. Consortia commonly run several at once: undergraduate scholarships, graduate fellowships, research seed grants, hardware or student-launch grants, and K-12 outreach money.
3. Check whether the consortium disburses only through **affiliate institutions**. Many do. Where your school is not an affiliate, find the nearest one and ask about a partnership before you write anything.
4. Find the **campus Space Grant representative** at your institution. Nearly every affiliate has one, usually a faculty member, and they generally know how the money moves. That conversation is worth more than the application text.
5. Ask, before you build the budget, whether this award carries a **cost-share or matching** requirement, and get the answer from the consortium rather than from any summary of the programme.
6. Ask what the consortium must report to NASA about the students your project touches, and write your outcomes so they hand it over ready-made.

## How to frame an amateur radio project here

Consortia fund aerospace-relevant student work, and amateur radio maps onto that honestly rather than cosmetically. Say it in their vocabulary:

- A satellite ground station is **telemetry, tracking and command**, and it supports cubesat operations.
- A high-altitude balloon payload with an APRS tracker is **atmospheric science plus telemetry**.
- Ionospheric propagation work is **space weather**, which sits squarely inside the programme's remit.
- All of it is **student workforce development**, which is the programme's own statutory purpose.

State student involvement in headcounts and hours. Consortia report upward on students reached, and an application that hands them that number in the shape they need is an easier application to fund.

## Common failure

Writing to "NASA Space Grant" as though it were one funder. Every rule that matters to your application — what it funds, when it closes, who may apply, what it wants reported — belongs to your own consortium, and none of it is on this page. Quote your consortium's own page in your notes, with the date you read it, so that a claim you build the budget on can be traced back to something somebody published.
